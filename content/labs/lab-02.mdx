<Step n={6} title="Part B: equivalence by brute force" time="10 min" id="part-b">

`counterexample(f, g, n)` walks through every row of `n` bits and returns the **first** row where `f` and `g` disagree, or `None`. It is the [equivalence checker](/weeks/week-03#logical-equivalence) from the notes, in eight lines.

```python
from itertools import product
for row in product([1, 0], repeat=2):
    print(row)          # (1, 1), (1, 0), (0, 1), (0, 0)
```

Then `equivalent(f, g, n)` is one line: two formulas are equivalent exactly when there is **no** counterexample.

<Terminal cmd="python circuits.py" out={`...
p→q ≡ ¬q→¬p ? True
p→q ≡ q→p   ? counterexample: (1, 0)`} />

</Step>

<Step n={7} title="Part C: adders from gates" time="20 min" id="part-c">

Use **only your gate functions**: the checks reject `+`, `-`, `//` and `%` in the adders.

1. `half_adder(a, b)` returns `(sum, carry)`: sum is XOR, carry is AND. One line.
2. `full_adder(a, b, cin)` returns `(sum, carry_out)`. Either use the two formulas in the docstring, or chain **two half adders and one OR** (the circuit on the [week 3 page](/weeks/week-03#adding-binary-numbers)).
3. `add_binary(x, y)` adds two bit strings like a CPU does:
   - pad the shorter string with `"0"` on the left (`str.zfill` helps),
   - walk the columns **from right to left** with `reversed(...)`, starting with carry `0`,
   - each column: `s, carry = full_adder(int(a), int(b), carry)`,
   - if a carry is left at the end, it becomes an extra leading `"1"`.

<Terminal cmd="python circuits.py" out={`half_adder(1, 1) = (0, 1)
11 + 6 = 10001 (binary)`} />

The checks add **all 256 pairs** of 4-bit numbers with your adder. If one pair fails, the message tells you which.

</Step>

<Step n={8} title="Part D: NAND is enough" time="10 min" id="part-d">

Rebuild NOT, AND, OR and XOR using **only calls to `NAND`**: no other gates, no `and`/`or`/`not`, no operators, no `if`. The checks read your code to make sure. The identities are in the notes:

$$
\neg p \equiv p \uparrow p, \qquad p \land q \equiv (p \uparrow q) \uparrow (p \uparrow q), \qquad p \lor q \equiv (p \uparrow p) \uparrow (q \uparrow q)
$$

For XOR, start with `n = NAND(a, b)`; four NAND calls are enough.

</Step>

<Step n={9} title="Part E: a Knights and Knaves solver" time="15 min" id="part-e">

Open `puzzles.py`. A **world** says who is a knight: `{"A": True, "B": False}`. A statement is a function of the world:

```python
"A": lambda w: w["B"]                  # A says: "B is a knight"
"B": lambda w: w["A"] != w["B"]        # B says: "We are of opposite types"
```

`solve(people, statements)` tries **every** world and keeps the consistent ones. The one rule from the lecture: a speaker is a knight **if and only if** their statement is true, so for every speaker `s`, `w[s] == statements[s](w)` must hold.

1. Loop over `product([True, False], repeat=len(people))`.
2. Turn each tuple into a world with `dict(zip(people, values))`.
3. Keep the world if the rule holds for **all** speakers (`all(...)` is ∀ again).

</Step>
