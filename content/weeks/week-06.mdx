## From truth to reasoning

For four weeks we have been asking one kind of question: **is this statement true?** We built truth tables, simplified formulas and checked quantified statements against data. This week we ask a different and, in a sense, more important question: **does this statement follow from those?**

The difference matters because most of what we know, we know by reasoning rather than by checking. A doctor who reads a test result, a detective who studies a crime scene, a programmer who reads an error message and a mathematician who writes a proof are all doing the same thing. They start from statements they accept and move, one careful step at a time, to a statement they did not know before. The steps they are allowed to take are called **rules of inference**, and there are surprisingly few of them.

Recall the argument we could not handle in week 4:

> All humans are mortal. Socrates is a human. Therefore, Socrates is mortal.

Everyone agrees that it is correct, and by the end of this page you will be able to say precisely **why**: which rule turns the first two sentences into the third.

## Arguments and validity

> [!definition] Argument
> An **argument** is a list of statements. All of them except the last are called **premises** (or hypotheses); the last one is the **conclusion**. We often write the conclusion after the symbol $\therefore$, read “therefore”.

> [!definition] Valid argument
> An argument is **valid** if the conclusion **must** be true whenever all of the premises are true. In other words, with premises $p_1, p_2, \dots, p_n$ and conclusion $q$, the argument is valid exactly when
> $$
> (p_1 \land p_2 \land \dots \land p_n) \to q
> $$
> is a tautology.

Read the definition slowly, because it says less than it seems to. Validity does **not** say that the premises are true, and it does not say that the conclusion is true. It says that the conclusion is **guaranteed** by the premises: if you accept the premises, you are forced to accept the conclusion. Validity is a property of the **form** of the argument, not of the facts it talks about.

Here is a valid argument with a false conclusion:

> If the moon is made of cheese, then pizza grows on trees. The moon is made of cheese. Therefore, pizza grows on trees.

Nothing is wrong with the reasoning; the problem is the premise. Logicians call an argument **sound** when it is valid **and** all of its premises are true. A sound argument always has a true conclusion. In this course we focus on validity, because that is the part logic can check.

### Checking validity with a truth table

Since validity means “$(p_1 \land \dots \land p_n) \to q$ is a tautology”, we can always decide it with a truth table. In fact we only need to look at some of the rows. A conditional can only be false when its hypothesis is true, so the rows that matter are those in which **every premise is true**. We call them the **critical rows**. The argument is valid exactly when the conclusion is true in every critical row. A single critical row with a false conclusion is a counterexample, and it shows the argument is invalid.

The truth table method always works, but with $n$ variables it needs $2^n$ rows, and an argument with twenty variables would need more than a million. Mathematicians and computers therefore use a second method, which scales much better: a chain of small steps, each justified by a rule we already know to be valid. Those rules are the subject of the next section.

## The rules of inference

Each rule of inference is a small valid argument that we have checked once and for all. Because it is valid, we may use it anywhere, with any statements in place of $p$, $q$ and $r$. There are eight rules for propositional logic. Here they are in one table; after it, we look at each one carefully.

| Rule | From | Conclude | Tautology behind it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Modus ponens | $p$, $\;p \to q$ | $q$ | $(p \land (p \to q)) \to q$ |
| Modus tollens | $\neg q$, $\;p \to q$ | $\neg p$ | $(\neg q \land (p \to q)) \to \neg p$ |
| Hypothetical syllogism | $p \to q$, $\;q \to r$ | $p \to r$ | $((p \to q) \land (q \to r)) \to (p \to r)$ |
| Disjunctive syllogism | $p \lor q$, $\;\neg p$ | $q$ | $((p \lor q) \land \neg p) \to q$ |
| Addition | $p$ | $p \lor q$ | $p \to (p \lor q)$ |
| Simplification | $p \land q$ | $p$ | $(p \land q) \to p$ |
| Conjunction | $p$, $\;q$ | $p \land q$ | $(p \land q) \to (p \land q)$ |
| Resolution | $p \lor q$, $\;\neg p \lor r$ | $q \lor r$ | $((p \lor q) \land (\neg p \lor r)) \to (q \lor r)$ |

### Modus ponens

*Modus ponens* (Latin for “the mode that affirms”) is the most natural rule of all, and we use it many times a day without noticing. If we know that $p$ implies $q$, and we learn that $p$ actually happened, then $q$ happened too.

$$
\frac{p \qquad p \to q}{\therefore\; q}
$$

“If it snows, I will study discrete math. It is snowing. Therefore, I will study discrete math.” In software it is the shape of every rule that fires: “If the password is correct, the user is logged in. The password is correct. Therefore, the user is logged in.”

### Modus tollens

*Modus tollens* (“the mode that denies”) runs the same conditional backwards. If $p$ would force $q$, and $q$ did **not** happen, then $p$ cannot have happened either.

$$
\frac{\neg q \qquad p \to q}{\therefore\; \neg p}
$$

“If it snows, I will study discrete math. I will not study discrete math. Therefore, it is not snowing.” This is the contrapositive from week 2 at work: $p \to q$ is equivalent to $\neg q \to \neg p$, and modus tollens is modus ponens applied to that contrapositive. It is also the debugger's favourite rule: “If the bug were in the database layer, the integration tests would fail. They pass. So the bug is not in the database layer.”

### Hypothetical syllogism

This rule chains two conditionals into one. If $p$ leads to $q$, and $q$ leads to $r$, then $p$ leads to $r$.

$$
\frac{p \to q \qquad q \to r}{\therefore\; p \to r}
$$

“If it snows, I will study discrete math. If I study discrete math, I will get an A. Therefore, if it snows, I will get an A.” Notice that the conclusion is itself a conditional: we have not learned whether it snows or whether you get an A, only that the first leads to the second.

### Disjunctive syllogism

When one of two options must hold and one is ruled out, the other remains.

$$
\frac{p \lor q \qquad \neg p}{\therefore\; q}
$$

“I will study discrete math or English literature. I will not study discrete math. Therefore, I will study English literature.” This is the logic of elimination, and every multiple-choice exam you have taken has trained you in it.

### Addition, simplification and conjunction

These three rules look almost too obvious to name, but they do essential bookkeeping inside longer arguments.

**Addition** lets us weaken a statement by adding an alternative: from $p$ we may conclude $p \lor q$ for **any** $q$. If “I will study discrete math” is true, then “I will study discrete math or I will visit Las Vegas” is certainly true as well, whatever happens with Las Vegas.

**Simplification** takes one part of a conjunction: from $p \land q$ we may conclude $p$. If I will study discrete math and English literature, then in particular I will study discrete math.

**Conjunction** combines two established statements into one: from $p$ and $q$ we may conclude $p \land q$.

### Resolution

The last rule looks the least familiar, yet it is the one computers love most.

$$
\frac{p \lor q \qquad \neg p \lor r}{\therefore\; q \lor r}
$$

The idea is a case split. Either $p$ is true or it is false. If $p$ is false, the first premise gives us $q$. If $p$ is true, then $\neg p$ is false and the second premise gives us $r$. Either way, $q$ or $r$ holds. The variable $p$, which appears once plainly and once negated, simply **cancels out**.

“I will study discrete math or databases. I will not study discrete math, or I will study English literature. Therefore, I will study databases or English literature.” We will see in a moment why this one rule is so important for automated reasoning.

## Building an argument step by step

With these rules we can now prove that a conclusion follows from a set of premises **without** writing a truth table. We write a numbered list. Every line is either a premise or follows from earlier lines by a named rule. When the conclusion appears as a line, we are done.

### An afternoon by the lake

This is the classic example from the lecture slides. Suppose we know the following four things.

1. It is not sunny this afternoon, and it is colder than yesterday.
2. We will go swimming only if it is sunny.
3. If we do not go swimming, then we will take a canoe trip.
4. If we take a canoe trip, then we will be home by sunset.

We want to show: **we will be home by sunset.**

The first step is always translation. Let $p$ be “it is sunny this afternoon”, $q$ “it is colder than yesterday”, $r$ “we will go swimming”, $s$ “we will take a canoe trip” and $t$ “we will be home by sunset”. Remember from week 2 that “$r$ only if $p$” means $r \to p$. The premises become

$$
\neg p \land q, \qquad r \to p, \qquad \neg r \to s, \qquad s \to t,
$$

and the conclusion we want is $t$.

Before writing anything, it helps to think backwards from the goal. We want $t$. The only premise that mentions $t$ is $s \to t$, so it would be enough to know $s$. The only premise that gives $s$ is $\neg r \to s$, so it would be enough to know $\neg r$. Where could $\neg r$ come from? From $r \to p$ by modus tollens, if we knew $\neg p$. And $\neg p$ is sitting inside the first premise. Now we can write the argument forwards:

| Step | Statement | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $\neg p \land q$ | premise |
| 2 | $\neg p$ | simplification, from 1 |
| 3 | $r \to p$ | premise |
| 4 | $\neg r$ | modus tollens, from 2 and 3 |
| 5 | $\neg r \to s$ | premise |
| 6 | $s$ | modus ponens, from 4 and 5 |
| 7 | $s \to t$ | premise |
| 8 | $t$ | modus ponens, from 6 and 7 |

Eight short lines replace a truth table of $2^5 = 32$ rows. You can confirm the result with the **canoe trip** preset in the checker above.

> [!tip] How to find an argument
> Work **backwards** from the conclusion to plan, then write the argument **forwards**. At each stage ask: which premise could give me what I need, and what would I need to know first in order to use it? This is exactly how mathematicians plan proofs, and it is how the backward-chaining part of this week's lab works.

### A debugging argument

The same technique organizes the search for a bug. Suppose that during an incident you establish three facts.

1. The bug is in the parser or in the database layer.
2. If the bug were in the database layer, the integration tests would fail.
3. The integration tests pass.

Let $a$ be “the bug is in the parser”, $d$ “the bug is in the database layer” and $f$ “the integration tests fail”. The premises are $a \lor d$, $d \to f$ and $\neg f$.

| Step | Statement | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $d \to f$ | premise |
| 2 | $\neg f$ | premise |
| 3 | $\neg d$ | modus tollens, from 1 and 2 |
| 4 | $a \lor d$ | premise |
| 5 | $a$ | disjunctive syllogism, from 3 and 4 |

The conclusion is that the bug is in the parser. Notice how much stronger this is than a hunch: as long as the three facts are right, the conclusion cannot be wrong.

## Two famous fallacies

A **fallacy** is an argument that looks valid but is not. Two of them are so common, and so convincing, that they have names. Both misuse a conditional.

**Affirming the conclusion.** “If you do every exercise, you will learn discrete math. You learned discrete math. Therefore, you did every exercise.” The form is

$$
p \to q, \quad q \quad \therefore\; p,
$$

and it is invalid: $(q \land (p \to q)) \to p$ is false when $p$ is false and $q$ is true. You may have learned discrete math in some other way, perhaps by reading the notes very carefully. The conditional only promises what happens **when** $p$ holds; it says nothing about other routes to $q$.

**Denying the hypothesis.** “If you do every exercise, you will learn discrete math. You did not do every exercise. Therefore, you did not learn discrete math.” The form is

$$
p \to q, \quad \neg p \quad \therefore\; \neg q,
$$

and it is invalid for the same reason. Run both through the checker above and look at the row that breaks them.

> [!warning] The fallacy that costs engineers the most time
> “If the server is down, users see an error page. Users see an error page. So the server is down.” This is affirming the conclusion, and it sends whole teams to restart a server that was never the problem. An error page has many possible causes. The valid direction runs the other way: if users see **no** error page, then (by modus tollens) the server is not down.

## Resolution: the rule computers love

Resolution has a remarkable property. If we first rewrite all premises as **clauses**, that is, as disjunctions of variables and their negations such as $p \lor \neg q \lor r$, then resolution is the **only** rule we need. Every other rule can be reproduced by resolution steps on clauses.

For example, modus ponens becomes resolution as soon as we write $p \to q$ in its equivalent form $\neg p \lor q$:

$$
\frac{p \qquad \neg p \lor q}{\therefore\; q}
$$

Here $p$ and $\neg p$ cancel, leaving $q$. A computer that only knows how to cancel complementary literals can therefore carry out any propositional argument. To show that a conclusion follows, it adds the **negation** of the conclusion to the premises and keeps resolving. If it ever derives the empty clause, a clause with nothing left in it, the premises together with “not the conclusion” are contradictory, so the conclusion must follow. This strategy, proof by refutation, is at the heart of many automated theorem provers, and its descendants power the SAT solvers we met in week 3.

## Rules of inference for quantified statements

The rules so far handle propositions. For statements with quantifiers we need four more rules, which let us move between “for all” or “there exists” and a particular element. They are simple, and each one matches something we already do informally.

**Universal instantiation (UI).** From $\forall x\, P(x)$ we may conclude $P(c)$ for any particular element $c$ of the domain. If all dogs are cuddly and Fido is a dog, then Fido is cuddly.

**Universal generalization (UG).** If we can show $P(c)$ for an **arbitrary** element $c$, one about which we assumed nothing special, then we may conclude $\forall x\, P(x)$. This is what a mathematician does with the words “let $n$ be any integer”. The important condition is “arbitrary”: proving $P(7)$ does not prove $\forall x\, P(x)$.

**Existential instantiation (EI).** From $\exists x\, P(x)$ we may conclude $P(c)$ for **some** element $c$, as long as we give it a new name and assume nothing else about it. “Someone got an A in the course; let us call that person $c$.”

**Existential generalization (EG).** From $P(c)$ for a particular element $c$, we may conclude $\exists x\, P(x)$. If Michelle got an A, then someone got an A.

| Rule | From | Conclude | Condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Universal instantiation | $\forall x\, P(x)$ | $P(c)$ | $c$ is any element of the domain |
| Universal generalization | $P(c)$ | $\forall x\, P(x)$ | $c$ is arbitrary |
| Existential instantiation | $\exists x\, P(x)$ | $P(c)$ | $c$ is a new name, about which nothing else is assumed |
| Existential generalization | $P(c)$ | $\exists x\, P(x)$ | $c$ is some element of the domain |

### Socrates, at last

We can now say exactly why the oldest argument in logic is valid. Let $H(x)$ be “$x$ is a human” and $M(x)$ “$x$ is mortal”.

| Step | Statement | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $\forall x\,(H(x) \to M(x))$ | premise |
| 2 | $H(\text{Socrates}) \to M(\text{Socrates})$ | universal instantiation, from 1 |
| 3 | $H(\text{Socrates})$ | premise |
| 4 | $M(\text{Socrates})$ | modus ponens, from 2 and 3 |

Steps 2 and 4 are used together so often that the combination has its own name. **Universal modus ponens** says: from $\forall x\,(P(x) \to Q(x))$ and $P(a)$, conclude $Q(a)$. In the same way, **universal modus tollens** says: from $\forall x\,(P(x) \to Q(x))$ and $\neg Q(a)$, conclude $\neg P(a)$.

### A longer example

Here is a second example from the slides, which uses all four quantifier rules. The premises are “a student in this class has not read the book” and “everyone in this class passed the first exam”. We want to conclude “someone who passed the first exam has not read the book”.

Let $C(x)$ be “$x$ is in this class”, $B(x)$ “$x$ has read the book” and $P(x)$ “$x$ passed the first exam”. The premises are $\exists x\,(C(x) \land \neg B(x))$ and $\forall x\,(C(x) \to P(x))$, and the goal is $\exists x\,(P(x) \land \neg B(x))$.

The plan is natural: take the student who has not read the book, give them a name, show that they passed the exam, and conclude that such a person exists.

| Step | Statement | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $\exists x\,(C(x) \land \neg B(x))$ | premise |
| 2 | $C(a) \land \neg B(a)$ | existential instantiation, from 1 ($a$ is a new name) |
| 3 | $C(a)$ | simplification, from 2 |
| 4 | $\forall x\,(C(x) \to P(x))$ | premise |
| 5 | $C(a) \to P(a)$ | universal instantiation, from 4 |
| 6 | $P(a)$ | modus ponens, from 3 and 5 |
| 7 | $\neg B(a)$ | simplification, from 2 |
| 8 | $P(a) \land \neg B(a)$ | conjunction, from 6 and 7 |
| 9 | $\exists x\,(P(x) \land \neg B(x))$ | existential generalization, from 8 |

> [!warning] Instantiate the existential first
> Notice the order in steps 2 and 5. We used existential instantiation **before** universal instantiation. If we had first instantiated the universal premise with some $c$ and then the existential one, we could not have reused the name $c$, because existential instantiation requires a **new** name about which nothing is assumed. The safe habit is: instantiate existential statements first, then apply universal statements to the names you created.

## How machines draw conclusions

Rules of inference are not only a tool for humans writing proofs. They are also how a computer can reason about facts it was given. The simplest and most widely used method is called **forward chaining**.

A rule engine keeps two things: a set of **facts** that are known to be true, and a list of **rules** of the form “if these facts hold, then that fact holds”. Forward chaining repeats one simple step: look for a rule whose conditions are all known facts, and add its conclusion as a new fact. That step is modus ponens, combined with conjunction to gather the conditions. The engine stops when a full pass over the rules produces nothing new.

Consider a small course advisor:

```python
facts = {"passed_math1", "passed_programming"}
rules = [
    ({"passed_math1"}, "can_take_discrete"),
    ({"passed_programming", "can_take_discrete"}, "can_take_algorithms"),
    ({"can_take_algorithms"}, "can_take_data_structures"),
]

changed = True
while changed:                                  # repeat until nothing new appears
    changed = False
    for conditions, conclusion in rules:
        if conditions <= facts and conclusion not in facts:   # all conditions are known
            facts.add(conclusion)               # modus ponens
            changed = True

print(sorted(facts))
```

Here the first pass already learns all three new facts, because each rule happens to come after the rule it depends on: `can_take_discrete` makes the second rule fire, and that makes the third one fire. The second pass learns nothing, and the loop ends. Had the rules been listed in the opposite order, the engine would have needed three passes to reach the same result, which is why the loop runs until nothing changes instead of a fixed number of times. Every fact in the final set is justified by a chain of modus ponens steps back to the starting facts, exactly like the numbered arguments above. In the lab you will write this engine yourself, ask it to explain **why** it concluded something, and add the opposite strategy, backward chaining, which starts from a goal and works back towards the facts, the way we planned the lake argument.

## Summary

An argument is valid when its conclusion must be true whenever all its premises are true, which is the same as saying that “premises imply conclusion” is a tautology. Validity concerns the form of an argument, not the truth of its parts; a valid argument with true premises is called sound. Validity can be decided by checking the critical rows of a truth table, but long arguments are better built as a chain of steps, each justified by a rule of inference.

The eight propositional rules are modus ponens, modus tollens, hypothetical syllogism, disjunctive syllogism, addition, simplification, conjunction and resolution. Resolution alone suffices once premises are written as clauses, which is why automated provers rely on it. Two tempting forms, affirming the conclusion and denying the hypothesis, are invalid. For quantified statements, universal and existential instantiation move from a quantifier to a particular element, and the two generalization rules move back; universal modus ponens combines instantiation with modus ponens. Finally, a rule engine that applies modus ponens until nothing changes is a small but genuine reasoning machine.
